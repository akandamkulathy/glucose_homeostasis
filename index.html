A very small pool

The blood glucose pool is not a store. It is a delivery line that happens to hold a little fluid.

Circulating glucose
~57 kcal
Liver glycogen
~380 kcal
Adipose lipid
~135,000 kcal

At rest the body spends about 75 kcal every hour. The entire circulating glucose supply would be gone in roughly 45 minutes if nothing refilled it. The fat reserve, at the same rate, would last more than two months.

Size and access speed are different properties. Glucose is small but instantly deliverable. Fat is enormous but has to be mobilized, transported, and processed before any of it reaches a cell.

Why muscle glycogen never rescues blood sugar

Pre-lab Q-D

Liver and muscle store the same molecule, but only the liver can release glucose back into the blood. Muscle lacks the enzyme that frees glucose from its phosphorylated form, so muscle glycogen is spent where it sits.

Run Intense exercise in the control room and watch the two reserves diverge: muscle glycogen collapses while the liver reserve is barely touched. The same substance, two destinations, decided entirely by which tissue is holding it.

The hand-off at 24 hours

Lab Q2 · Q3 · Q6

Start a fast and watch the second ledger. For the first several hours the blood glucose pool is refilled mostly by liver glycogen. Plasma glucose falls about 20% and then stops falling.

By 24 hours the liver reserve is effectively empty — yet glucose holds steady. The refill has switched over to gluconeogenesis: new glucose assembled from glycerol released by fat breakdown, from amino acids, and from recycled lactate.

The plateau is not the absence of change. It is one supply line being replaced by another, at almost exactly the rate needed to hold the line.

The bottleneck that makes ketones

Pre-lab Q-F

Fatty acids arriving at the liver carry a great deal of energy, and the normal route to release it runs through metabolic convergence — the shared gateway into cellular respiration.

During a long fast the liver is running gluconeogenesis hard, and gluconeogenesis draws on the same machinery. The gateway narrows. Fatty acid energy arrives faster than it can pass through, backs up, and is repackaged into ketone bodies that leave the liver and supply everyone else.

Ketones are not waste. They are the overflow channel, and the brain learns to drink from it.

Two states, one pathway

Lab Q20 · Q22

Starvation and untreated type 1 diabetes both produce ketones through the identical pathway. Only one of them produces acidosis. Run both and compare:

Day 3 fast
K ≈ 23
pH 7.37
No insulin, 36 h
K ≈ 136
pH 7.12

Ketone production differs by well under a factor of two. Ketone concentration differs by six. The gap opens because the tissues that consume ketones saturate: past roughly 40 mg/dL, raising the supply no longer raises the rate of removal.

Below saturation, faster production is met with faster clearance and the pH holds. Above it, production has nowhere to go but the plasma. Insulin is what normally keeps the system on the safe side of that threshold.

Starving in a sea of fuel

Lab Q13 · Q22

Set insulin production to 0% while food intake continues. Plasma glucose climbs past 400 mg/dL — and the energy ledger shows cells burning fat and ketones, not glucose.

Skeletal muscle and adipose tissue need insulin to pull glucose across the membrane. Without it, glucose accumulates outside the cells that need it while those cells behave as though the body were starving. The liver, reading the same starvation signal, pours out still more glucose.

Now set insulin production back to 100% and watch every variable turn around together. One intervention, because one signal was missing.

What this model is and is not

This is a teaching model of energy flow. Reservoir sizes, hormone responses, and the direction and rough magnitude of every flow are physiological, and the fasting, exercise, starvation, and diabetic ketoacidosis behaviours reproduce the pattern you will see in the laboratory simulation.

It is not a clinical tool. Individual pathways are compressed into single arrows, the subject is a 70 kg adult at a fixed body temperature, and food arrives continuously rather than as meals. Use it to reason about direction, sequence, and magnitude — not to predict a patient.

Built as a study companion for the Regulation of Blood Glucose laboratory. Reservoirs hold energy; arrows are rates. Every value updates from a continuously integrated model of glucose, glycogen, lipid, protein, and ketone balance.
